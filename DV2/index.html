Cricket Australia, A Nation's Game.

From Bradman's invincibles to a packed Boxing Day Test, from the urn at Lord's to a sticky Hobart night under floodlights — this is a data essay on how Australia became the most successful side in cricket history, and how that dominance flowed home into the streets, suburbs, and stadiums of a country that just won't stop watching.

Australia have played international cricket for nearly 150 years. In that time, no other nation has won more World Cups, held the world No. 1 ranking longer, or built a domestic T20 competition that grew from a curiosity into one of the country's most-watched sporting events. This essay traces that story in four chapters — the global record, the tournament cabinet, the rivalry with England, and the rise of the Big Bash — and asks one question along the way: what does dominance actually look like in data?

01

Australia on the World Stage

To the teams that faced Australia in the 2000s — they often knew, by the coin toss, that they were playing for second. McGrath, Warne, Gilchrist, Ponting, the Waughs: a generation that didn't just win matches, they erased opposition from the contest before lunch on day one.

02

The Trophy Cabinet

Ten ICC trophies. Six ODI World Cups. Two Champions Trophies. A T20 World Cup in 2021 and, just to round it out, the inaugural World Test Championship in 2023. No other side has won more of any of these.

03

The Ashes

The oldest contest in cricket — fought over a tiny clay urn since 1882. Every Test ever played for it appears in the strip below. Green is Australia, navy is England, sand is a draw. Read it left-to-right and you can see the eras: the Bradman years, the West Indian interregnum of the 1970s, the rebuild of the 2010s.

A small fact for scale. The MCG holds 100,024 people — the second-largest cricket stadium in the world after Narendra Modi Stadium in Ahmedabad. On Boxing Day, it sells out by the time the kookaburras start calling.

04

The Big Bash

In December 2011 the BBL replaced state-based domestic T20 with eight city-based franchises and a mandate to fill stadiums in the summer holidays. By season seven it was averaging over 30,000 fans a game. Then came COVID. Then came the rebuild.

A Nation's Game

Australia's cricket story is unusual because the international dominance and the domestic mania aren't two separate things — one feeds the other. A T20 World Cup in 2021 fills BBL stadiums the following December. A loss in the Ashes sends a million viewers back to the highlights of 2006. The data, read end to end, describes a country that has organised a non-trivial portion of its summer around watching cricket — and has, more often than not, been rewarded for the attention.
